Abstract

Competitive humanoid table tennis requires two distinct abilities: executing a desired return and deciding where to return the ball. We separate these two problems, with the ball landing target serving as an interface between motor control and strategy. (1) We first train a whole-body motor policy conditioned on the desired landing target, which maps the target to an executable humanoid stroke through a model-based strike planner and a learned controller. (2) We then improve the motor policy through self-play in closed-loop rallies, where the robot learns to handle incoming balls generated by another player rather than only prescribed incoming balls. At this stage, the landing targets are still sampled without considering the opponent, so the policy improves its execution without learning game strategy. (3) Finally, we freeze the entire motor stack and train a stroke-level strategic policy to choose landing targets through competitive self-play.

Motor self-play improves both landing accuracy and legal-return rate during rallies. More importantly, with the entire motor stack fixed, changing only the target-selection policy leads to large differences in game outcomes: the learned strategy wins 89%, 82%, and 73% of points against center, random, and away-from-opponent baselines, respectively. The learned strategy also changes where it sends the ball according to the opponent's position and the previous target, showing that self-play learns more than simply aiming at fixed “good” locations. On hardware, the same frozen execution stack and strategic interface support complete 11-point games against a recreational human opponent, including multi-stroke rallies with both forehand and backhand returns.

Measurement details

Legal landing rate counts all attempts. Hardware accuracy is conditional on legal first landings and does not measure overall return success. Simulation uses a 30 cm accuracy threshold and hardware uses 45 cm; these rates are not directly comparable. Landing error is mean ± sample SD.

Sampling protocol

Each map uses 500 categorical samples on the same probability scale (0–0.3). Only opponent position or the previous target changes; all other inputs stay fixed. These tests show local sensitivity, without measuring either input's contribution to winning.
